First-Time Homebuyer Money in San Diego

Arranged for one situation: first-time buyer, school-district classified employee, one-person household, income under 80% of area median ($97,950), buying anywhere in San Diego County with the city preferred. Ordered by what that buyer should actually do, biggest money first, with the agency that runs each program linked. If that's not your situation, the programs are still real.

01

Pick the lender first — it gates every dollar below

Almost nothing on this page comes from an agency directly. Each program flows through its own network of approved lenders, and the networks only partly overlap: WISH runs through Federal Home Loan Bank member institutions holding 2026 allocation · CalHFA through CalHFA-approved loan officers · the County's program through SDHC's participating-lender list · GSFA through its own participating lenders · each bank grant only through that bank · NACA only through NACA. Pick a lender casually and you can lose $32,837 before you've seen a house.

So the first calls are lender interviews, starting with the 2026 WISH members that lend statewide — Golden 1, Patelco, Mechanics Bank, Kinecta, KeyPoint, University Credit Union — and the script is six questions:

  1. "Do you hold 2026 WISH allocation right now?" Members claim WISH funds individually and run out individually. Start with lenders that say yes.
  2. "Are you CalHFA-approved?" If yes, WISH and the CalHFA package can ride on one loan.
  3. "Are you on SDHC's participating-lender list?" That's the door to the County's 22% program — only relevant if the purchase ends up outside city limits.
  4. "Are you a GSFA Platinum participating lender, and which loan types keep the Select gift after August 31?"
  5. "Which of these can you combine on a single loan — including WISH on top of the County program — and what does the whole stack net?" No agency publishes the stacking answer; the lender is the one who can give it.
  6. "Do you do FHA on condos, including single-unit approvals — and with my credit score and debts, what price am I actually approved for?" Section 02 explains why both halves matter.

For an in-city purchase, yeses on 1, 2, 4, and 6 are the ones that matter; 3 counts only for a purchase outside the city. A slightly better rate at a lender who fails these questions is smaller than a lost grant.

02

What you can afford — the number that shapes the search

Your constraint is the monthly payment. The grants below cover the down payment — WISH alone ($32,837) is more than twice the FHA down payment on a $400,000 condo ($14,000). What limits the purchase is the payment a lender lets you carry: roughly 45% of gross monthly income, minus existing debts.

Gross incomeRough price band*FHA 3.5% down
$75,000~$280,000–$310,000~$10,000
$95,000~$380,000–$410,000~$14,000

*At ~6.5% and 45% debt-to-income, with modest other debts and condo taxes, insurance, and HOA included. Car payments and student loans subtract directly. Stronger credit (700+) lets some lenders stretch toward 50% debt-to-income, which adds roughly $50,000–$60,000. A pre-approval settles the real number.

A band like that means condos and townhomes, which carry three things worth knowing on day one: HOA dues count in the debt math dollar-for-dollar (a $400/month HOA removes roughly $50,000 of purchase price) · an FHA loan needs the condo project on FHA's approved list, so check before falling for a unit · the deed-restricted condos in section 03 are priced below this math, which is why they get a standing search.

The rest of the qualifying picture: credit — 640 minimum for the FHA route (700 if debt-to-income runs to 50%), 680 conventional. Cash you still need on hand even with every grant — the $8,209 WISH match, earnest money (~1% of price), inspections, the $100 education course, and the one-year home warranty CalHFA requires on resale purchases: plan on roughly $13,000–$14,000 of your own money. One caution for a public employee with student loans: a taxable grant (Wells Fargo's issues a 1099) raises adjusted gross income, which raises an income-driven loan payment, which shrinks the payment room above — weigh that before taking it.

03

Your money, biggest first

Funded for 2026

WISH — $32,837 grant for $8,209 of your own savings

Federal Home Loan Bank of San Francisco · through the member lender from section 01

A 4-to-1 match: you bring $8,209 (savings, gift funds, or sweat equity), the program adds $32,837. First-time buyers at or below 80% AMI — that's you. Homebuyer counseling required.

Per-lender allocation runs out even while the program is open — which is why section 01 comes first. A retention period applies: sell or refinance early and part of it comes back. $13M is funded for 2026; the member window runs through 3/12/2027.

Source: FHLBank SF — WISH

Standing search

Deed-restricted below-market condos — a lower price, not just help at closing

San Diego Housing Commission · 257 units in Black Mountain Ranch, Pacific Highlands Ranch, and North Park

A formula-capped purchase price on a specific unit. Because the discount lowers the price itself, it works directly on the payment limit from section 02. The same formula caps your resale price later — the discount coming in is traded for capped appreciation going out.

The tier trap: each unit carries its own income band, 60% up to 120% AMI, and the chart runs backwards in the middle — the 80% row ($97,950) is a higher dollar figure than both the 90% row ($82,450) and the 100% row ($91,650), because of a federal high-cost adjustment on the lower tiers. An income in the high-$80s or low-$90s clears "80%" but is over-income for a "90%" or "100%" unit. Check the tier on each listing, don't assume. Other requirements: two years living and working in San Diego County (confirm your dates), no home owned in three years, homebuyer education. There's no waitlist: watch MLS/Zillow/Redfin for "deed restricted" in those developments with pre-approval and education certificate already in hand — the first eligible applicant wins. North Park is the central location; the rest are far north.

Source: SDHC Affordable For-Sale Housing · AMI chart

Outside city limitsFunded

County DCCA — 22% of the price, deferred, plus a $10,000 grant

County of San Diego, administered by SDHC · unincorporated areas + Carlsbad, Coronado, Del Mar, Encinitas, Imperial Beach, La Mesa, Lemon Grove, Poway, San Marcos, Santee, Solana Beach, Vista

A deferred second for 22% of the purchase price (3% simple, no payments until sale/refinance) plus a $10,000 closing grant. Income ≤ $97,950, price ≤ $743,000. Because that 22% comes out of the first mortgage, it cuts the monthly payment — roughly $550/month at a $400,000 price — which makes it the one program on this page that materially raises the price band in section 02. Nothing this size is funded inside the city for this income band.

Goes through SDHC's participating-lender list (section 01, question 3). Tenant-occupied properties are ineligible at offer time, no exceptions. Counterintuitive trigger: paying off the first mortgage early calls this loan due. Chula Vista runs its own version — up to $120,000 — but has served roughly 15 families total since 2022, so treat it as a long shot. Whether WISH stacks on top is question 5 of the lender script.

Sources: County DCCA · SDHC status page (all jurisdictions)

Open now

CalHFA MyHome + the low-income rate lock

CalHFA · statewide, works everywhere above · through a CalHFA-approved lender

The FHA route: 3.5% of the price as a deferred second at 1% simple (due at sale/refinance/payoff), and under 80% AMI the discounted low-income rate lock and cheaper mortgage insurance come with it. On a $400,000 condo that covers the full $14,000 down payment.

The eHome 8-hour course ($100) plus its one-on-one follow-up is the only education CalHFA accepts — details on CalHFA's borrower requirements page. Skip the CalPLUS "Access"/"ZIP" add-ons unless closing cash is genuinely short: they buy extra cash with a ~0.75-point-higher rate for 30 years, which loses after year three.

Sources: MyHome · live rate sheet

Ask by Aug 31

GSFA Platinum Select — the school-employee gift

Golden State Finance Authority · through a GSFA participating lender

Up to 1.5% of the loan as a non-repayable gift (~$5,900 at a $400,000 price). "Select" occupations explicitly include public school district "administration and staff" — classified employees qualify by name, no first-time requirement.

The occupation eligibility survives August 31, 2026, but the eligible loan-type list narrows that day — ask a participating lender this week which loan types keep the gift (question 4 of the script). The other 4% of "up to 5.5%" is a repayable 15-year second with payments from day one; at this price band the gift is the point, usually skip the rest. The gift is "subject to market conditions" — a lender can quote less, so get two quotes.

Source: GSFA Platinum

All-or-nothing

NACA — the alternative to the whole stack

Neighborhood Assistance Corporation of America · remote counseling; nearest offices Hawthorne / Upland / Sacramento

No down payment, no closing costs, no mortgage insurance, and a below-market rate — 5.75% 30-year at Priority Member status, which an income under the county median ($130,900) gets automatically.

NACA is its own mortgage, so choosing it replaces everything else on this page — no WISH, no CalHFA, no DCCA, no bank grants. Once section 01's answers show what actually stacks, compare NACA's zero-cash, lower-rate loan against the best combined stack. NACA also caps the price by its own affordability math (strict on one income) and the counseling pipeline has no published timeline.

Source: NACA

04

Before signing anything

  1. Decide the agent-rebate question first — it's irreversible. The CSEA / Union Plus rebate (~$2,500 at this price band, paid as a check after closing) requires registering with the program before signing with any agent; sign first and it's gone for good. It requires their network agent, and it can't combine with Homes for Heroes — one lane or the other, decided before the first agent conversation.
  2. Geocode your current address once at the FFIEC map — two minutes. If it sits in a majority-minority census tract, U.S. Bank's Access Loan ($5,000 credit + up to $12,500 deferred + they pay the mortgage insurance) qualifies on where you live now and works nearly anywhere you buy. If not, cross it off.
  3. Get the education certificates early — eHome for CalHFA, SDHC's course if the County program is in play. Certificates in hand are what make the first-come deed-restricted listings winnable.
  4. Never let a rebate or a bank grant pick the lender. Section 01's answers outrank every incentive below them.
05

Bank grants — check per address, take at most one

Each requires that bank's mortgage, so taking one usually means giving up the WISH/CalHFA lender — a trade that loses five figures. These matter mainly if section 01 comes up empty or a candidate address happens to hit.

Bank of America — America's Home Grant: $7,500 toward closing costs or rate buydown, county-wide, no first-time requirement — checker. Chase Homebuyer Grant: $5,000 in eligible tracts (City Heights-type areas qualify), applied to rate buydown first — checker. Wells Fargo: $15,000 total at this income ($10,000 down payment + $5,000 closing credit), and your current address can satisfy the location test — but fixed-rate conventional only (no FHA, so it forfeits the CalHFA low-income route) and it's the taxable one from section 02 — details. Citi: $7,500 closing credit, likely available in San Diego (branch-area rule) — confirm with a Citi loan officer, starting from their program page. U.S. Bank: the geocode check in section 04.

06

Not for you, not fundable, or not real

Everything here still shows up in searches, agent conversations, and bank tools.

Real programs that don't fit this situation: H.O.P.E. ($40k educator grant) requires a teaching credential and a classroom in one specific city council district — classified staff are excluded (guidelines). SDHC Middle-Income ($40k loan + $10k grant) starts at an income floor near the 80%-AMI line; SDHC's program page and AMI chart disagree by about $5,000 on where exactly — if income is anywhere near the line, one call settles it, (619) 578-7788 (program page); otherwise skip. MIDA starts above 80% AMI. The city's Low-Income loan (17%/$125k + $10k — the richest in-city program when funded) is flatly "No Funding Available" with no waitlist; ask SDHC to flag you if it refunds (status page). Dream For All: portal closed, and it requires first-generation status — parents who never owned — plus a lottery (CalHFA). Mortgage Credit Certificates: no issuer has allocation (dry since 1/2025) — if a lender promises one, ask for the issuer and open allocation in writing (GSFA status). Habitat: no orientations currently scheduled, and its income floor is $61,250. Good Neighbor Next Door: teachers only, near-zero inventory. Prop 37 (Nov ballot): new-construction only, 2027 at the earliest. CalVet: veterans.

Dead or marketing: CalHFA teacher programs (STEAP / Extra Credit) — discontinued March 2020 by CalHFA's own bulletin, still the top search result for "California teacher home buying program"; a lender who pitches it is working from stale information. CalSTRS home loans (dead 2011) · CalPERS home loans (dead 2010, actively impersonated by marketers) · Landed (consumer program paused; no San Diego-area district is a partner) · Teacher Next Door — a private brokerage whose advertised "grants" are estimates with no named funding source (their own FAQ) · SD Foundation "Access Granted" $40k — out of funding per LISC, though Bank of America's tool still lists it · school-district employee housing projects — rental, unbuilt, years away; not homebuying help · San Diego Community Land Trust (defunct) · UCSD's mortgage program (UC faculty only) · BofA's $10,000 Down Payment Grant (Bay Area counties only). Throughout: check the administering organization's own page before believing a bank tool or a blog.

Figures come from the administering agencies' own pages, pulled August 17, 2026. Funding statuses change without notice — confirm with the agency before acting on any of this. Not financial advice.
