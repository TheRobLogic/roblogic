4. Your theory: "Kermit never actually RMO'd, so everything Cyberdyne built is illegal"

Verdict: partially right, but the mechanism works differently than you think. There IS a real legal issue here. It's called "qualifier-for-hire" under California Business & Professions Code §7068.1. But it cuts against your dad first, not Cyberdyne — because the qualifier has the duty to supervise. And it doesn't automatically void every contract Cyberdyne ever signed.

The way this actually works in California:

  • A "qualifier" (RMO or RMG) on a license has a legal duty to actively supervise the company's construction activity. Not just lend their name.
  • If the qualifier didn't supervise, that's a violation — and CSLB can come after both the qualifier (your dad) AND the license holder (Cyberdyne).
  • Penalties: CSLB accusation, license revocation, fines, restitution orders. Not automatic refunds to every customer.
  • "All their work is illegal" isn't quite right. The work still happened. The contracts aren't automatically void. But if a past Cyberdyne customer complains, they have strong standing if the qualifier wasn't really on the job.

You mentioned Kermit was paid $6,000 total for the whole 3-year stretch (2019–2022). That's roughly $167 a month. For context, a full-time qualifier actively running a busy contractor's operations is a serious role — typically $60K–$120K+ a year, or a meaningful equity stake, or both. A flat $6K over 3 years IS consistent with a paper-qualifier arrangement — which is exactly what §7068.1 was written to stop. If there's a case to be made, that compensation number is a key piece of it.

Practically: the §7068.1 angle is a pressure point on Cyberdyne — but pulling that lever also exposes your dad, because he signed on as the qualifier. If this is a path you and Kermit want to walk, you'd want a contractor-law attorney talking to CSLB together, not separately. CSLB has a voluntary disclosure process that's different from getting hit cold with an accusation.
