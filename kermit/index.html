The 1099 question — more nuanced than "workers' comp fraud." Cyberdyne's current job postings are 1099 contractor roles, not W-2 (WayUp and ZipRecruiter — Solar Appointment Setter, Solar Sales Consultant, Residential Solar Rep). 1099 independent contractors don't require the hiring company to carry workers' comp, so the "no employees" CSLB certification is technically consistent with 1099-only staffing.

The real question is whether California's ABC test (Labor Code §2775 / Dynamex / AB5) actually allows solar sales and solar installation to be 1099 work. Under prong B, a worker must perform work "outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business." Solar installers and solar salespeople working for a solar company flunk prong B almost by definition. If the classifications are wrong, the exposure is:
  • EDD misclassification audit — back payroll taxes + penalties
  • Labor Commissioner wage claims
  • Civil action (PAGA / class action)
Those are real enforcement pressure points, but they're an EDD/Labor Code issue — not the CSLB §7110/§7125.2 workers' comp fraud framing. Earlier draft had that wrong.

The B-only license question — and the C-10 sub

Cyberdyne's CSLB #1050614 is Class B only (general building). No C-10 electrical, no C-46 solar.

Per City of San Diego Information Bulletin 301 §II.B.7 (rev. Oct 2025), PV plans "may be stamped and signed by" a C-10, B, or C-46. 2. The "qualifier-for-hire" theory — corrected legal framework

Verdict: real legal exposure on both sides. Mechanism is narrower than my first draft implied. California Business & Professions Code §7068.1 says a qualifier (RMO or RMG) "shall be responsible for exercising supervision and control of their employer's or principal's construction operations." Violating that duty is a basis for CSLB discipline against both the qualifier and the license holder — plus a misdemeanor: up to 6 months jail and a $3,000–$5,000 fine. The statute applies to Kermit as qualifier AND to Cyberdyne as license holder.

How this actually works in California:

  • The qualifier has a legal duty to actively supervise the company's construction activity. Not just lend their name.
  • If the qualifier wasn't supervising, CSLB can come after the qualifier (Kermit) AND the license holder (Cyberdyne).
  • §7068.1(e) penalty: misdemeanor, up to 6 months jail, $3K–$5K fine. Plus CSLB administrative discipline (§7090 et seq.) — accusation, license suspension/revocation, restitution orders.
  • "All their work is illegal" isn't automatic. The work still happened; contracts aren't automatically void. But under the Wright v. Issak (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1116 line of case law, past customers can argue the license was "effectively unlicensed" for §7031(b) purposes — entitling them to recover 100% of what they paid. This is fact-intensive: the customer has to prove the qualifier wasn't actually supervising. And it's subject to a 1-year statute of limitations, so the window is narrow.

The $6,000 total for the entire 3-year stretch (2019–2022) is roughly $167/month. A full-time qualifier running a busy contractor's day-to-day operations is a meaningful role — industry comp is typically $60K–$120K+ a year or a real equity stake. A flat $6K over 3 years is consistent with a paper-qualifier arrangement, which is what §7068.1 is designed to prevent. That compensation fact is load-bearing evidence for the qualifier-for-hire theory.

Practical reality: the §7068.1 angle is a pressure point on Cyberdyne, but pulling that lever also exposes Kermit — because he was the qualifier. If this is a path you and Kermit want to walk, you'd want a contractor-law attorney advising both of you (probably separately — your interests and Cyberdyne's may diverge). CSLB doesn't publish a formal "voluntary disclosure" program, but walking in represented with documentation is a very different posture from getting hit cold after a customer complaint.
