What it is

A California Type 48 license: On-Sale General, Public Premises. That is the bar license. Beer, wine, and spirits by the drink, no food requirement, no one under 21 inside. It comes with a Type 58 caterer's permit, which transfers along with it. The license is active, carries no conditions, and has no disciplinary history, which you can confirm yourself on ABC's license lookup.

It can be moved to any premises in San Diego County. Not out of the county.

Why a used license is the way in. San Diego County is over the state's quota for general licenses, so ABC issues new ones only through an annual lottery. In 2026 the whole county gets 9 new on-sale general licenses, the fee is $19,840, the odds are long, and a lottery license cannot be resold for two years. Transfers of existing licenses are exempt from the quota. That is what you are buying: the ability to open a bar in the city without winning a lottery.

If your concept has a kitchen: a Type 48 can be exchanged for a Type 47 (restaurant, full bar, minors allowed) for a $125 ABC fee. A 47 has to operate as a real eating place, with a kitchen and meals. The exchange also changes who decides one of the approvals below, which can matter.

How a move works

A license is issued to a person and an address. There is no way to buy it and hold it while you look for a space. You name the site on the application. The good news is that ABC handles the change of owner and the change of address in one application, and premises still under construction are allowed: ABC approves the transfer and holds the certificate until the room is ready.

  1. Get site control

    A signed lease, or something the ABC district office will accept as a right to occupy. The application asks for the landlord, the rent, the lease expiration, and a floor plan with the licensed area outlined.

  2. Check the room against the City rules below

    Size, zone, neighborhood, and whether the address had a bar license recently. This decides whether the City part takes a couple of weeks or a year.

  3. Open escrow

    California requires the full purchase price to be deposited with a liquor-license escrow company before the application is filed, and the seller is paid only after ABC approves. That rule protects you: no money changes hands until ABC says yes. Escrow companies in the county that do this every week include Allison-McCloskey, Oakwood, and Warranty Escrow.

  4. File with ABC

    One application covering both the new owner and the new address (ABC calls it a "premises and person" transfer). Fee $1,565. Every owner of the buying entity gives a personal history, fingerprints, and a financial affidavit. A notice of the transfer is recorded with the County Recorder first; the escrow company handles that.

  5. File with the City

    Every ABC applicant in the City of San Diego also files a Zoning Use Verification ($718.43). ABC will not finish until the City has answered.

  6. The public notice period

    A notice is posted at the door of the new site for 30 straight days. If the address is not currently licensed, neighbors within 500 feet get a mailed notice and it runs in a newspaper. The police department, the City Council, City planning, and the District Attorney are notified and can object or ask for conditions (hours, security, that kind of thing).

  7. ABC investigates, then approves

    ABC's own figure is 55 to 65 days on average, 30 days minimum by law, and up to 95 days or more if someone objects. When it approves, escrow closes, the license is yours, and you can pour. Until then the room stays dark: there is no temporary permit for a license that is changing address.

How long, all in: roughly three to four months from a signed lease if the room is an easy one, five to seven if the neighborhood requires the extra police review described below, and a year or more if the room needs a City hearing. Finding the room is on top of that.

Two ABC rules that shape the paperwork. The binding purchase agreement gets signed close to the filing date; ABC will not process a transfer that fulfills an agreement more than 90 days old. And a license can never be used as collateral or to secure a promise, so "option to buy later" structures are out. Both are why the order above is: room first, then agreement, escrow, and filing together.

Picking the room

The room decides almost everything about time and cost. The City of San Diego has no bar-specific alcohol ordinance; a bar is an ordinary "eating and drinking establishment" under the zoning code. What matters:

Under 5,000 square feet, in a commercial zone

In the CC and CR commercial zones a bar under 5,000 square feet is allowed by right: no hearing, no conditional use permit, no community group vote. Over 5,000 square feet it becomes "nightclubs and bars over 5,000 square feet," which needs a conditional use permit from the Planning Commission. The City's own estimate for those is 10 to 12 months and real ones have run longer. Stay under the line.

A room that had a Type 48 in it recently

If the address was licensed and operating with a Type 48 within the last 90 days, state law switches off the whole "too many licenses in this area" review (the PCN below). A recently closed bar is the fastest possible site. A room that had a restaurant license (Type 47) is second-generation too, but the 90-day exemption reads "same type," so ask the district office how they treat it; exchanging to a 47 is one clean answer.

Live music near housing

If the lot touches a residential lot, some zones cap hours at midnight (the Mid-City neighborhoods and Old Town, and CN neighborhood-commercial zones). Separately, a room built as a stage-and-audience venue for 25 or more can be classified as an "assembly and entertainment" use, and next to residential zoning that classification caps hours at 10 p.m. (11 on Fridays and Saturdays). Existing band bars are treated as bars with music on the side. A brand-new stage in a space that was never a venue is where that question gets raised, so take a room that has already been a music room, or get the City's read in writing before you spend on improvements.

Downtown is the hard way

Downtown (the Centre City district) a bar under 5,000 square feet needs no City alcohol permit, but any amplified band or DJ needs a conditional use permit, plus a paid acoustical study if there is housing on or next to the site, which downtown always is. The Gaslamp is stricter: a bar without made-to-order food during all hours needs a conditional use permit at any size, and amplified music needs another. For a live-music bar, look elsewhere.

Zones that say no

  • CO office zones (alcohol is barred as a primary use, so Mission Valley's office corridors are out).
  • Industrial zones: the heavy IH zones do not allow bars at all, and the IS zone near the Casbah caps a new bar at 1,000 square feet. The Dot and the Casbah are grandfathered.
  • Old Town's OTMCR zones, and anything over 5,000 square feet in Old Town.
  • The CU-1 zones in Mid-City: no spirits and no live entertainment.

Three things that are easier than you think

  • Parking. Inside the City's transit priority areas the parking minimum is zero, so converting a storefront needs no new spaces. Most of central San Diego is inside one; the Adams Avenue and South Park corners tested were not, so check the address.
  • Community planning groups are advisory. They can talk; they cannot stop a by-right use.
  • Noise is a rule you can plan for: 65 dB at the property line for a commercial neighbor, 40 dB after 10 p.m. for a single-family neighbor, 75 dB any hour next to industrial. After 10 p.m., music "plainly audible at fifty feet from the building" is a violation on its own. Industrial or all-commercial surroundings are the friend of a loud room.

The PCN, explained

ABC checks whether the new site's census tract has more bar licenses per person than the county average, or sits in a police reporting district with 20 percent more crime than the city average. If either is true, the tract is "over," and for a Type 48 the City has to find that "public convenience or necessity" (PCN) would be served before ABC can approve.

In the City of San Diego that finding is an SDPD investigation: you pay $2,728 at the City Treasurer with ABC's form 245, the police have up to 90 days, and a denial can be appealed within ten days. If the City never answers inside the 90 days, ABC decides itself and by law cannot hold the City's silence against you.

On the license-count test, most central tracts are already over. The exceptions found were City Heights (four tracts) and one tract in Barrio Logan. The crime half of the test is not published; the ABC district office gives you both numbers for any address.

Two ways around it. A room that had a Type 48 operating within the last 90 days skips the PCN entirely. And if you exchange to a Type 47, the PCN case is made to ABC directly rather than to the City, which is a lighter process.

What you will need to have

  • An entity (an LLC is typical) formed and in good standing. Every owner is on the application.
  • Funds. Escrow has to hold the full price before filing, so a lender or partner has to be ready at filing, not at closing.
  • The site: lease or accepted right to occupy, landlord contact, floor plan, and the City zoning verification.
  • Personal history and fingerprints for each owner (ABC's forms plus Live Scan).
  • A liquor-license escrow company and, ideally, an ABC licensing consultant. Former ABC investigators do this work for buyers and know the San Diego district office's habits. Rob can point you to one.
  • Your ABC and bar history, if any. ABC asks.

The name "The Bancroft," the artwork, and any equipment are separate conversations, sold by contract with no ABC involvement. Say what you want and it can be part of the same deal or not.

Straight answers

Can I buy it now and figure out the location later?
No. ABC issues a license to a person and an address, and every form asks for the premises. Under construction is fine; undecided is not.
Can I open while ABC processes?
No. The temporary permit only exists when the license stays at the same address. A moved license opens when ABC approves.
Can I pay Rob directly and skip escrow?
No. State law puts the full price in escrow before filing and pays the seller after approval. It is the buyer's protection as much as anything.
Can I put an option on it or make a deposit and lock it up for a year?
Not the way people usually mean. ABC will not process a transfer fulfilling an agreement more than 90 days old, and the license cannot secure a promise. Deals are structured around signing close to filing. An ABC-experienced attorney papers this.
Do I need a kitchen?
Not for a Type 48. Only if you exchange to a 47.
Can it go to La Mesa, Chula Vista, National City?
Anywhere in San Diego County. Each city has its own zoning rules; this page covers the City of San Diego.
Does the name come with it?
Separate item, separate conversation. So is the gear.
Who do I call?
Rob, and then the ABC San Diego district office at 8620 Spectrum Center Blvd, Suite 302, (858) 300-6855, which will tell you the tract numbers for any address you are considering.
