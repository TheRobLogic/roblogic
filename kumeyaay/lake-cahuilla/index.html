Lake Cahuilla

Lake LeConte · the Blake Sea

A freshwater lake that periodically filled the Salton Trough whenever the Colorado River avulsed west. Seven highstands in 2,500 years; the last one drained within living memory of the Kamia.

The Cycles

The Salton Trough was periodically filled by freshwater Lake Cahuilla whenever the Colorado River avulsed west across its delta rather than emptying into the Gulf of California. Rockwell et al. (2022) document seven highstands in the past 2,500 years, six of them in the last 1,100 years.1

  • ~5 BCE–612Early highstand
  • 930–966Highstand
  • 1007–1070Highstand
  • 1118–1241Long-duration highstand
  • 1486–1503Highstand
  • 1618–1636Highstand
  • 1731–1733Last fill; rapid desiccation

Maximum stand was approximately +13 m above modern sea level (~43 ft). The lake extended roughly 105 miles north–south and 35 miles east–west — comparable in surface area to Lake Tahoe and reaching well south of the present US–Mexico border into the Mexicali Valley.

The Bathtub Ring

The travertine "bathtub ring" along the base of the Santa Rosa Mountains, visible from Interstate 10 between Indio and the Salton Sea, is mineral precipitated at the lake's old waterline. The most-photographed exposure is at Travertine Point near Mecca.

The modern Salton Sea — created in 1905 by a Colorado River canal breach — sits roughly 70 m below sea level, well under the prehistoric lake's footprint.

The Kamia Along the Shore

The Kamia branch of the Kumeyaay lived in the eastern desert and southern Salton Trough, with territory extending well south of the present US–Mexico border into Mexicali.

Unlike the coastal Kumeyaay (acorn and shellfish economy), the Kamia were floodplain horticulturalists — growing corn, beans, and squash when Colorado overflow fed the New and Alamo Rivers. Their material culture and farming practice were closer to the River Yumans (Quechan, Mojave, Cocopa) than to the western Kumeyaay coastal bands.

When the lake refilled, fish-camp villages and weirs were built or rebuilt along the new shore; when it desiccated, populations reorganized around remaining springs and the Colorado overflow channels.

Fish traps

Approximately 650 stone fish weirs are mapped along the southwestern shoreline and at Travertine Point near Mecca. The traps follow the lake's recessional terraces — concentric arcs of bowl-like depressions and U or V-shaped rock alignments. They caught bonytail chub, razorback sucker, and Colorado pikeminnow.2

The youngest fish-camp use dates to approximately AD 1700, consistent with protohistoric Kamia exploitation of the final highstand.

Today

The Salton Sea occupies the deepest part of the old lake bed. The geology is interpretable from the highway, the museums, and the parks:

  • Travertine Point, off CA-86 — the type locality, where the bathtub ring is clearest
  • Lake Cahuilla Veterans Regional Park, La Quinta — modern recreation lake at the prehistoric highstand elevation, gives a sense of where the waterline was
  • Imperial Valley Desert Museum, Ocotillo — interprets the 10,000-year human record of the region
  • Anza-Borrego Desert State Park — the western edge of the old lake
