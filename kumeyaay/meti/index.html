VI · Sacred Place

Metí

Neti · El aguaje de San Jorge · Spring Valley village

A Kumeyaay village at a year-round spring in what is now Spring Valley, San Diego County. Baptized October 1775, dispersed after the revolt. The Bancroft Ranch House sits on the same parcel today.

Spring Valley, San Diego Co.

The Village

The pre-contact Kumeyaay village at the spring is documented under two transcriptions: Metí (more common in Spring Valley Historical Society and SOHO materials) and Neti (used by local archaeologists). Thomas J. Adema's Our Hills and Valleys: A History of the Helix–Spring Valley Region (San Diego Historical Society, 1993) opens with the village under "Metí, generally known to local archaeologists as Neti."1

"Jamacha" (Hamacha) is a separate Kumeyaay placename a few miles east near present-day Jamacha Boulevard. There is no credible support for that name or for "Si'kwa" at this specific site.

The Spring

Spanish padres named the spring El aguaje de San Jorge — St. George's Spring — in October 1775 when they baptized members of the rancheria at the water source.2

The spring was a year-round source in an otherwise arid landscape. It is the reason the Kumeyaay village existed and, much later, the reason for the Anglo "Spring Valley" name. Today the spring presents as a perennial seep rather than a flowing source. Per Spring Valley Historical Society President Robert Case, it still seeps.

Worth noting precisely: the spring lies on the Bancroft County Park parcel (3554 James Circle) — administratively separate from the adjoining Bancroft Ranch House Museum parcel (9050 Memory Lane). The two parcels were historically part of the same Helix Farms estate but today are managed separately and have no direct access between them.

Pre-Contact Occupation

Hard evidence places Kumeyaay-ancestor occupation at the site at at least ~1,300 years — projectile points dated to approximately AD 700 have been recovered.3 SVHS broader language ("water for two thousand years") is plausible for Late Prehistoric occupation but is not a directly cited radiocarbon date.

Documented material culture from the site: arrowheads, soapstone (steatite) cooking vessels, obsidian and volcanic-glass flakes, and trade shells. A Pacific Coast Archaeological Society Quarterly study (Sasson et al.) places Netí within a half-day foraging radius of the coast — shellfish and an eider scapula on site indicate a substantial settled rancheria rather than a seasonal camp.

San Diego State University ran a field school at the site in the early 1970s under archaeologist Dr. Tim Gross, a Spring Valley native.

1775

The October 1775 baptisms at the spring were followed within weeks by the Kumeyaay revolt of November 5, 1775, in which Father Luis Jayme was killed at Mission San Diego de Alcalá. About 600 warriors from 40+ rancherias attacked the mission.

Local tradition — held by SVHS and SOHO — is that Metí residents participated in the revolt and the village was largely abandoned afterward to avoid Spanish reprisal. The standing academic source on the revolt, Carrico's "Sociopolitical Aspects of the 1775 Revolt" (San Diego History journal, 1997), documents the wider list of rancherias but does not single out Metí by name.4 The Metí-participation claim should be treated as strongly held local tradition rather than direct mission-record evidence.

"Spring Valley"

The English name Spring Valley was proposed in 1866 by Rufina Porter, daughter of property owner Capt. Rufus King Porter. It refers to the physical spring, not to the Kumeyaay name Metí/Neti. The settler-era naming was independent.

The Bancroft Ranch House

The two-room adobe on the parcel was built in 1863 by Judge Augustus S. Ensworth on a 160-acre claim that included the spring. It was the first non-Indigenous dwelling in eastern San Diego County. Construction used curved timbers salvaged from the wreck of the Clarissa Andrews in San Diego Bay.

Succession

  • 1863Ensworth builds the two-room adobe.
  • 1865Capt. Rufus King Porter buys for $400; adds kitchen, dining room, two bedrooms.
  • 1866Rufina Porter proposes "Spring Valley" as the area's name.
  • Oct 1885Hubert Howe Bancroft (the historian, 1832–1918) buys the property and calls it "Helix Farms." Estate grows to ~500 acres, one of the largest olive ranches in southern California. Bancroft was San Francisco-based and used the property as a summer retreat.
  • 1905Bancroft's main 60,000-volume library transferred to UC Berkeley — still there today as The Bancroft Library.
  • 1940Bancroft heirs sell to the Spring Valley Chamber of Commerce.
  • 1967Spring Valley Historical Society takes title.

Today

The Bancroft Ranch House Museum at 9050 Memory Lane is open Friday, Saturday, and Sunday from 1:00 to 4:00 pm (free, volunteer-run, 619-469-1480). The original two-room adobe houses bound volumes of Bancroft's 39-volume Works, Kumeyaay material from the site, and pioneer memorabilia.

The adjacent Bancroft County Park at 3554 James Circle contains the Bancroft Rock House (a small fieldstone structure built ~1885–1888 from Porter's stone fences, used as fireproof manuscript storage) and the spring itself. Native plants Kumeyaay used — Olney's bulrush, laurel sumac, cattail, mesa bushmallow, elderberry — still grow on the grounds.

No federally recognized Kumeyaay band publishes a formal land acknowledgment naming Metí/Neti specifically as their ancestral village, though Sycuan, Jamul, Viejas, Barona, and Campo all descend from the broader Kumeyaay homeland that includes the Sweetwater drainage.
