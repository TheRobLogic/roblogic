A Reference Document

Kumeyaay

'Iipay · Kamia · Tipay · the people of this place

An Indigenous people of southern California and northern Baja California — their land, their tradition, and what the public record holds about both.

Orientation

The Kumeyaay are an Indigenous people whose homeland extends from the Pacific coast east to the Colorado River, north to roughly Escondido, and south past Ensenada in Baja California — a territory that the United States–Mexico border bisects without erasing.

Pre-contact population is estimated by ethnographer Florence Shipek at 16,000–19,000, well above Kroeber's earlier figure of ~3,000. Thirteen federally recognized bands occupy reservations in San Diego County today. Four communities operate in Baja.

The nation divides dialectically into three branches:

  • 'Iipay — northern (Mesa Grande, Santa Ysabel, San Pasqual, Inaja-Cosmit)
  • Kumeyaay / Kamia — central and eastern desert (Campo, La Posta, Manzanita; the lakeshore people of the Imperial Valley)
  • Tipay — southern, including four bands in Baja California (San José de la Zorra, San Antonio Necua, La Huerta, Juntas de Nejí)

"Diegueño" is the Spanish-mission exonym now largely retired in favor of self-designations. "Anasazi" is a Navajo word for a different (Ancestral Puebloan) people and should not be applied here.

Topics

I · Tradition

Bird Songs

A musical migration map
~300 songs, all-night ceremonial cycles with gourd rattle, geography encoded in song order. Shared across Yuman peoples and adopted by Cahuilla.
 II · Cosmology

Origins

Avi Kwa Ame, Tuchaipa & Yokomatis
The regional Yuman emergence at Spirit Mountain, and the Kumeyaay creation cycle of the twin brothers who raised the land from salt water and instituted ceremony.
 III · Geology

Lake Cahuilla

Seven highstands in 2,500 years
The prehistoric freshwater lake that periodically filled the Salton Trough, last in 1731. The Kamia branch lived as floodplain horticulturalists along its shore.
 IV · Geography

Trans-Peninsular Trails

Coast to Colorado
The continuous trail network that unified Kumeyaay territory. Shells and steatite moved east; obsidian, salt, and mesquite moved west. Interstate 8 follows the same corridor today.
 V · Sacred Place

Jacumba Hot Springs

Emergence at the spring
Where the creator twins Tuchaipa and Yokomatis emerged from the ground. ~10,000 years of occupation; active border-wall construction at the site in 2025–26.
 VI · Sacred Place

Metí · Spring Valley

El aguaje de San Jorge
A Kumeyaay village at a year-round spring in what became Spring Valley, San Diego County. Baptized October 1775, dispersed after the revolt that November. The Bancroft Ranch House sits on the same parcel today.
 VII · Sacred Place

Cuchumá

The exalted high place
The shaman training mountain on the California–Baja border. First NRHP site ever listed for Native sacred significance (1992); under active dynamite blasting for the second border wall as of April 2026.

The framing question

For most of the twentieth century, popular accounts of the Indigenous Southwest described peoples who had vanished, civilizations lost, mysteries left in the desert. The Kumeyaay were never lost. Their descendants are alive, govern federally recognized reservations and Mexican comunidades, teach the language, sing the bird songs, and have been on this land continuously since the Late Pleistocene.

This site is a reference. It collects what the public record holds — ethnographic, archaeological, geological, journalistic — and links to the primary sources. It is not a substitute for Kumeyaay self-representation, and it tries to mark clearly where knowledge stops being public.

What this site does not contain

Specific song-by-song place sequences of the bird-song cycle, portions of the Keruk/mourning cycle, certain spring and pictograph locations on Cuchumá and elsewhere, and shaman-training particulars are intentionally held by initiated culture-bearers and are not published. Their absence here is deliberate. The right way to engage deeper is at public bird-sing gatherings (Barona, Sycuan), the Kumeyaay Community College at Sycuan, or the Iipay Nation of Santa Ysabel and Viejas cultural programs.
