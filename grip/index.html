What's possible. What isn't.

A recon pass: what already exists for tracking grip gear, why none of it fits a working freelancer, and what survives once we send the haters in.

tl;dr

Nothing on the market is built for a freelance key grip with natural-language input. Selling our own version as a SaaS doesn't survive scrutiny. Building it for self-use does. The other four tabs above are the actual build dossier in four reading levels.

Why the gap is real

  • Wrap = lost gear. 2am, you throw it in the truck, the missing C-clamp becomes an L&D charge a week later.
  • L&D liability with no docs. Freelancers eat damage costs they can't prove weren't pre-existing.
  • Multi-truck attribution. On a 3-truck show, nothing tracks which truck the 40" C-stand is on. The best boy IS the database.
  • Sub-rentals slip through. Gaffer rents from a competitor, gets returned to the wrong house at wrap.
  • Pull-list vs truck reality. Hand-counting C-stands at 6am.

Methodology

This page is the output of two passes through the problem.

Pass 1 — landscape research

Four parallel research agents (all Claude Opus, web-enabled) sent out to map the territory: existing software, what working grips actually do today, the technical build options if we make it ourselves, and the closest AI/voice inventory tools in adjacent industries.

Pass 2 — hater agents

Once the build pitch had a shape, we sent four adversarial "hater" agents in parallel, each told to attack one vulnerability surface and find every reason the plan fails. No balance, no steelmanning — just looking for cracks.

  • Tech-reality hater — STT accuracy, offline behavior, hallucination, push reliability, battery drain
  • Market hater — TAM, distribution channels, buyer culture, industry contraction
  • Unit-economics hater — LLM cost per user, churn, CAC vs LTV, solo-founder bandwidth
  • Legal / IP hater — defamation, two-party consent, FRE 803(6), App Store policy, IATSE rules

What survives both passes is what's actually worth doing. The rest is below.

Why we're not selling it

The four haters came back from different directions and pointed at the same answer: this is not a SaaS.

Tech reality

STT word-error-rate on grip jargon ("Cardellini," "Mafer," "1.2k Joker") is ~20% even with vocabulary biasing. Every miss feeds a confident-looking hallucinated DB write. Voice-assistant research is unambiguous — three retries at 2am and the user is on Notes.app forever. iOS silent push is throttled by design and blocked entirely in Low Power Mode, which crew triggers by 6pm.

Market reality

TAM is ~15–25k working grips & best boys in North America. Realistic paid-sub ceiling: 300–800 = $72–192k ARR at peak. Closest historical competitor (Griplist): dead since Jan 2021. Cinematographers pay once, not monthly. FilmLA 2025 shoot days down 16% YoY — lowest non-pandemic year on record.

Unit economics

Heavy user gross margin: negative on every plan we modeled. LTV ~$48 vs CAC $80–200, ratio 1.6 (need 3+). Solo-founder support burns out around month 14–20 at 150–300 users. The math doesn't close.

Legal exposure

Hallucinated writes naming coworkers = defamation suit. California §632 two-party consent = $5k per voice captured in the background. FRE 803(6) makes AI-transcribed records inadmissible in exactly the L&D disputes the product was supposed to win. Production NDAs cover gear lists — founder personally on the hook to every studio his customers work for.

What survives — the self-host build

Strip the SaaS dream and every landmine in the section above disappears.

  • No paying customers → no defamation exposure on third parties.
  • No App Store review → no AI rejection cascades.
  • Own data only → no NDA breach, no two-party consent issue (you speak, you consent).
  • No L&D evidence claim in TOS → no FRE 803(6) trap.
  • Self-hosted → no LLM-per-user cost spiral, just your own bill.
  • No support burden, no churn, no CAC, no support pings at 2am.

The recommendation

  • Build it for yourself. PWA, your own API key, your own box.
  • Self-host. Data stays on your computer, never goes to a third party.
  • Open-source it if you want. No liability since you're not selling. Builds your rep in the grip community.
  • Don't pitch it as an L&D evidence tool. Frame as personal kit organizer + loan tracker. Every legal landmine in this brief traces back to the L&D positioning.

Gripventory

A small app you run on a computer at your house that tracks your gear. You talk to it. It writes things down. Nobody else sees it.

What it does

The first time, you walk your kit and tell the app what you have, snapping photos as you go. After that, you just talk to it like a person. "Marco took the C-stand kit to truck B." The app understands the sentence and updates your list. It pings your phone when something you loaned out has been gone too long. You reach it from set through a private connection — it never sits on the public internet.

The parts

  • A code editor with AI built in (Cursor or Claude Code) — the tool you use to build the app.
  • Python — the language the AI writes the app in, you mostly read it.
  • A simple web server framework called FastAPI — one file, easy to follow.
  • A small database file (SQLite) — your data lives here; copy the file to back it up.
  • A web page that installs on your iPhone like a real app.
  • An AI "brain" (Claude) — turns your sentences into updates to the list.
  • A free push notification service (ntfy.sh) — for the reminder pings.
  • A private network tool (Tailscale) — lets your phone reach the home computer from anywhere.
  • GitHub — where the source code is kept; open so others can use it too.
  • An always-on computer at your house — whatever you've got, even an old laptop.

How it gets built

  1. Install the editor, make a GitHub account, get an API key from Anthropic.
  2. Ask the AI to write the basic skeleton — a list of items, a place to add new ones.
  3. Type in your own gear and your slang ("stinger" is a particular cable, etc.).
  4. Wire up the part that reads your sentences and changes the list.
  5. Add the reminders for loans.
  6. Move the whole thing to your home computer and set up the private network.
  7. Use it on a job, see what breaks, ask the AI to fix it.

Gripventory

It's a magic notebook. You talk to it. It writes things down for you. It lives in your house.

What it does

You point at every piece of gear once and tell the notebook what it is. The notebook listens. After that, you just say things like "Marco took the dolly" and it remembers. When you ask "where's the dolly?" it tells you. When Marco still has the dolly three days later, the notebook taps your phone on the shoulder: "hey, Marco still has the dolly."

The parts (don't sweat the names)

  • A robot helper that writes computer code for you — you tell it what you want, it types the code.
  • A box that runs the notebook — a computer at your house, always on.
  • A secret tunnel so your phone can talk to the box from anywhere (Tailscale).
  • A brain inside the notebook that understands sentences (we rent it for pennies).
  • A bell that rings on your phone when something needs your attention.

How you build it

  1. Get the robot helper installed on your computer.
  2. Tell it: "make me a notebook with pages for my stuff" — it writes the code.
  3. Write down all your gear in the notebook.
  4. Hook up the brain so it understands what you say.
  5. Hook up the bell.
  6. Move the whole thing to the box at your house and open the secret tunnel.
  7. Try it on a job — when something is dumb, tell the robot helper to fix it.

Money

A few bucks a month. Less than coffee. Most of the parts are free.

The recipe

The recipe for the notebook lives on the internet for anyone to read. Rob can read along too and help when you're stuck. The stuff inside the notebook — your gear — only you can see. That part stays in your house.
