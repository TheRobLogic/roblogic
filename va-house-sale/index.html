What to do, in order. One step at a time.

If there was no will and you are his only heir, the house became yours the day he died. A filing by mail, under $100, puts that on the record; that is the "deed in my name" part. Each step below names two people to call. All were checked on 8 September 2026; details at the bottom.

Before anything else

The lawyer and the clerk will ask for: exact date of death; will or no will; whether he was married when he died; other children, including from another marriage; any mortgage, home-equity loan or reverse mortgage; whether he was on Medicaid after age 55; who has keys, and whether anyone has stayed in the house since. If he was married when he died and she is the mother of all his children, she inherits everything under Virginia law. Call the lawyer before spending anything.

This week

1

Get more death certificates

Order four more certified copies from wherever you got the first one. You will be asked for one at several of the steps below.

Done when you have five copies.

2

Insure it as an empty house

Ask an agent for a vacant-dwelling policy in your name (or the estate's; the agent will say which). Tell them the owner died and the house is empty. Whatever policy your father had may not pay on an empty house, and freeze damage is excluded once it sits four days without heat or drained pipes.

6

Get the appraisal before anything is touched

An appraiser values the house as of the date of death. That number becomes your tax basis, so selling near it means little or no tax. Nothing gets cleaned out, repaired or removed until this report exists. Ask for a retrospective appraisal as of the date of death, for estate and basis purposes; the keyholder lets them in.

7

Call the probate clerk

8

File the two forms. This is the deed in your name.

The lawyer fills in the List of Heirs, CC-1611 and the Real Estate Affidavit, CC-1612; you can also print them yourself. Sign both in front of any California notary and mail them to the clerk with the fee: $25 state fee, possibly a matching $25 county fee, plus about $18 recording; the clerk quotes the total. This is the no-will route, open once 30 days have passed since the death with nobody appointed to run the estate. Once recorded, you are the owner of record and the tax account moves to your name.

Both forms list every heir. If you have brothers or sisters, or your father was married when he died, read "If it is not that simple" below before filing.

Done when the clerk sends back the recorded copies.

Selling

11

When you can close

Virginia gives your father's creditors one year from his death to reach the house, so a title company usually will not insure a sale by an heir inside that year. If the anniversary is close, close after it. If it is not, the lawyer has two ways around it: qualify you as administrator and have the debts settled on the record, or negotiate an indemnity with the title underwriter. If he was on Medicaid, the lawyer also gets the state's written estate-recovery determination before you sign a contract.

Done when the lawyer confirms the earliest insurable closing date in writing.

12

Call all three listing agents. Best listing window is April to June. Auction is the other route; it gives you a firm end date and suits a house with no clean comparables.

After closing

15

Taxes

Keep three documents: the appraisal, the closing statement, and the 1099-S. Your cost basis is the date-of-death value, so a sale near it is little or no gain. Virginia withholds nothing at closing; you sign a registration form (R-5) instead. You file a Virginia nonresident return (Form 763) and your California return; California taxes the gain and Virginia gives a credit for it, so file California first. Your father's final 2026 returns are due April 15 and May 1, 2027. A CPA or enrolled agent who files in both Virginia and California does this from those three documents.

Done when the returns are filed.

Only if you need it

If it is not that simple
  • You have brothers or sisters. Each of you owns a share. All of you sign the two forms and the sale deed. One holdout means a court partition, six to eighteen months.
  • He was married when he died. If she is the mother of all his children, she inherits everything and you have nothing to sell. If he had a child who is not hers, she takes one third, the children two thirds, and she has rights ahead of creditors. Use the estate lawyer.
  • There is a will. It has to be recorded with the clerk, and Virginia charges probate tax when it is, about $200 to $270 here. If it leaves the house to you outright, you sell as the person named in it. If it tells an executor to sell, that executor has to qualify, the clerk's website says in person, and an out-of-state executor posts a bond unless a Virginian qualifies alongside. Use the estate lawyer and ask about a Virginia co-executor.
  • The deed says joint with survivorship, life estate, or transfer on death. The house may have skipped the estate entirely. The lawyer reads the deed and tells you in one call.
  • You end up signing as administrator, not as owner. A California notary cannot certify that capacity on the deed; the lawyer uses California's all-purpose acknowledgment or a Virginia online notary.
Traps
  • Closing inside one year of the death. Not insurable against his creditors.
  • A reverse mortgage. Thirty days to answer the lender's notice, and the notice goes to the empty house.
  • Medicaid. The state can recover against anything he held an interest in at death. Get the written determination before you sign a contract.
  • Any loan on the house. Foreclosure can need as little as 14 days' notice, and it goes to the recorded heirs. The List of Heirs is what puts you on that list.
  • Deed fraud. The alert in step 4 catches it.
  • Wire fraud. Every digit confirmed by phone at a number you looked up yourself.
  • Insurance. Vacant after 30 days empty with the contents out; freeze loss excluded after four days without heat unless the pipes were drained.
  • Cleaning out before the appraisal. The basis evidence is gone for good.
  • Anyone staying there with verbal permission. It forfeits the 14-day eviction hearing. Nobody gets a key without a signed receipt.
  • "We buy houses" offers. Nine to fourteen percent under resale in published studies. Decline.
  • A power of attorney. Skip it unless you will be unreachable; title underwriters reject them often.
